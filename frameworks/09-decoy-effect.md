# Decoy Effect (Asymmetric Dominance)

**Origin:** Huber, Payne and Puto (1982). Popularised by Dan Ariely's Economist subscription example.

**Evidence note:** Well replicated in lab settings; real-world effect sizes vary. A/B test before rolling out.

## Core idea
Adding a third option that is clearly worse than one option (the target) but similar to it makes the target look better. The decoy is not meant to sell much.

Example:
| Option | Price | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Single | $30 | 1 unit |
| Double (decoy) | $55 | 2 units |
| Triple (target) | $60 | 3 units |

The double makes the triple look like the obvious choice.

## E-com levers
- Bundle tiers (1, 2, 3 pack) with the target bundle marked "best value"
- Subscription vs one-off with a mid option that is less attractive than subscribe
- Size or variant ladders
- Shipping options (standard, express, priority) where one is the clear pick
- Free shipping thresholds paired with suggested add-ons

## When to use
- Raising AOV
- Steering to higher-margin or subscription options
- Building pricing pages and bundle offers

## Diagnostic questions
1. Which option do we want most people to choose, and why (margin, retention, AOV)?
2. Is the decoy clearly dominated by the target on the dimension shoppers care about?
3. Is everything still honest? (Real prices, real savings, no fake "was" prices.)
4. What does the test show for AOV, conversion rate and margin together?

## Common misuse
- Too many options (choice overload).
- Fake reference prices. Illegal in many markets.
- Optimising AOV while conversion drops.

## Pairs well with
4Ps (Price), TPB (attitude toward value), Zeigarnik (threshold progress), Racecar Lubricants (increase prices, conversion).
