# Pygmalion Effect

**Origin:** Rosenthal and Jacobson (1968), "Pygmalion in the Classroom". Higher expectations from others lead to higher performance.

**Evidence note:** The original study is contested and effect sizes in later research are small and context-dependent. In marketing, the practical application is identity and expectation framing. Use it as a creative prompt and test results. Do not present it as a proven conversion law.

## Two uses in e-com

### 1. Customer-facing: identity and expectation framing
Treating customers as the capable, discerning people they want to be encourages behaviour that matches.
| Lever | Example |
|---|---|
| Identity labels | "For people who read the label", "Insiders", "Founding members" |
| Status tiers | Loyalty levels that signal expertise or commitment |
| Expert positioning | Content that assumes intelligence instead of over-simplifying |
| Community roles | Ambassadors, reviewers, beta testers, early access |
| Onboarding expectations | "Most members see X after Y" (only when true and evidenced) |

### 2. Team-facing: managing the people who build the store
High, explicit expectations for agencies, freelancers and team members, paired with support, tend to improve output. Useful when the skill is used for team planning.

## When to use
- Loyalty and community programme design
- Brand voice and copy tone
- Retention and advocacy campaigns
- Briefing teams and partners

## Diagnostic questions
1. What identity does the customer want to hold by buying this?
2. Does our copy talk up to them or down to them?
3. Do our loyalty tiers reward behaviour we want (reviews, referrals) or only spend?

## Common misuse
- Flattery that feels fake or exclusive language with no real benefit.
- Outcome claims ("you will see results") that are unsubstantiated. This is a regulatory risk in health, finance and regulated categories.
